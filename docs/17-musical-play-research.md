# Musical play: experiments, musical intent, and listening gates

Research date: October 17, 2026. Recommendation: test **Phrase Rider**, **Groove Fighter**, and **Ribbon Lead** as three independent ways to perform music. Their common unit should be a continuing musical idea, with intentional silence and variation. More action should change an idea's energy rather than repeatedly replace its beginning. These are design hypotheses for listening tests, not claims that theory guarantees a pleasant result.

## What is wrong with the current gesture model

At the start of this investigation, `packages/music-core/src/arcade.ts` generated at most one quarter note per action. Calm strike/dash/leap used only the first two entries of a four-note contour. Both notes could then snap to the same observed accompaniment pitch. Repeated hits therefore produced many disconnected starts, even when the source motif had a more interesting rhythm and ending. Borrowing the first four source pitches did not preserve source note durations or rests. The user's report of unsatisfying two-note melodies follows directly from that design; it is not evidence that more complicated harmony inference is required first.

Keep the useful existing contracts from [11-arcade-music-theory.md](11-arcade-music-theory.md): one shared transport, deterministic editable notes, audible-note evidence before scale fallback, retained patch identity, and no invented chord progression. Extend the expressive time scale and give the performer ownership of continuation, change, and release.

## Candidate matrix

| Candidate | Player controls | Musical engine and distinct benefit | Main risk / scope |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Phrase Rider** | Hold to continue; choose a motif; steer lift/settle; change intensity and instrument | A one-bar rhythm/contour bank with authored rests, repeated with controlled variation; instant coherent material | Must distinguish shaping a phrase from queuing its replacement. Low–medium implementation cost |
| **Groove Fighter** | Latch a groove; add hats/ghosts; punch a fill or break | Stable kick/snare structure continues while fills decorate the tail and return to the groove; action becomes arrangement | Duplicate backing drums and constant fills can obscure the groove. Medium cost |
| **Ribbon Lead** | Vertical pitch region, horizontal energy or direction, hold/release gate | One continuous scale-aware melodic voice; stepping, holding and resting are direct actions | Pointer jitter, retrigger chatter and pitch snapping can remove player intent. Medium cost |
| Tempo / Pulse Pilot | Tap a pulse before start; later conduct faster/slower with bounded boundary changes | Tapping sets the whole song's BPM before playback; a future transport could change backing and performance together at a declared bar boundary | Tempo changes need shared tick/audio-clock mapping and stored timing decisions. Pre-start tap tempo is modest scope; live conducting is higher scope |
| Echo Duel | Play/choose a short call, then answer it | Alternating call/rest/answer; player can repeat, invert or settle the reply | Requires explicit turn timing and honest distinction between player and generated answer. Medium cost |
| Phrase Gardener | Select a seed; grow/shrink, prune, repeat, replace ending | Persistent phrase changes one dimension at a time, retaining recognizable rhythm and contour | Less immediate physical excitement; mutation must remain reversible. Medium cost |
| Conductor Arena | Move between low/high energy zones; cue entrances and drops | Existing bass, harmony, drums and melody are arranged by density and register | More arranging than composing; requires authored coherent layers and clear lane capture. Medium–high cost |
| Orbit Arpeggiator | Hold a pitch region; orbit controls order/rate/octave; release inserts breath | Rhythmic movement through observed chord tones or an explicit chosen pitch set | A single bass note does not supply a full chord; automatic arpeggios can become repetitive. Medium cost |
| Rhythm Sketch / Pitch Paint | Tap a rhythm first, then draw its pitch contour | Decouples two difficult decisions; timing can be expressive before pitch is chosen | Two-stage interaction is slower, but especially promising for keyboard-only users. Medium cost |
| Musical Grapple | Throw a line to an audible target pitch, then reel through steps | Destination-first melody: predictable landing with playful path and controlled leaps | Target should be shown as a pitch/anchor, not falsely as a detected cadence. Medium–high cost |

The first three test authored coherence, rhythmic performance, and direct melodic agency with little conceptual overlap. Do not add all ten before learning which feels playable. Echo Duel and Phrase Gardener are especially good second experiments if Phrase Rider sounds pleasant but feels too automated.

Tempo / Pulse Pilot preserves the user's idea that drumming can establish or conduct timing. Initially, taps could establish a global BPM while stopped, then start every layer on that tempo. Later, bounded changes could take effect at a visible bar boundary, with the whole backing following the same transport. Changing only the lead's local playback speed against a fixed backing would change their synchronization rather than the song's tempo. Initial Groove Fighter therefore varies subdivisions, accents and density inside the shared pulse; this is an implementation scope decision, not a claim that tempo play is musically invalid.

Weapons could also select **phrase packs or ensembles**: a heavy weapon might offer a bass-and-drum groove pack, while a staff selects sustained melodic calls. That is closer to Conductor Arena and could be a separate explicit experiment. It changes the current timbre contract: selecting a weapon would choose musical material or multiple roles as well as sound. If tested, name the pack and its lanes visibly, let players preview it, and preserve all performed parts when keeping the take. The current three-mode experiment keeps mode/phrase intent separate from instrument selection so those control models can be compared honestly.

## Musical rules worth testing

**Identity before novelty.** A motive is a recognizable musical idea that can be developed. Preserve an audible combination of rhythm, contour and accent across repetitions. A small bank might contain an arch, a rising question, a descending answer and a syncopated hook; changing a bank entry should be an explicit phrase choice. Repetition with one altered ending is more legible than changing every note on every action. This is a product interpretation of motive development, not a universal recipe. [Puget Sound: Motive](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/MotiveSection.html).

**Keep rhythm, pitch, emphasis and articulation independently controllable.** Intensity can add a passing subdivision, strengthen an accent or shorten a gate while retaining the motif's structural notes and rests. Do not make every fierce phrase a continuous sixteenth-note stream. Ableton's Rhythm generator separately exposes step count, pattern placement, density and step duration; that is a useful precedent for separable controls, not evidence that any chosen density sounds good. [Ableton: MIDI Tools](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/midi-tools/).

**Let the accompaniment guide destinations without erasing the route.** Prefer nearby sounding harmony pitches for selected strong arrivals, allow short scale steps between them, and avoid snapping every note of a two-note idea to the same anchor. Non-chord tones have approach/departure behavior; merely belonging to the scale does not make a note harmonically consonant. Short passing/neighbor motion is a bounded heuristic. Source motifs can still contain stylistically intentional dissonance. [Puget Sound: Non-Chord Tones](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/NonChordTonesIntroduction.html).

**Continue phase when steering.** Queue a new phrase at a visible boundary, or change the current phrase's upcoming notes while keeping its musical position. Repeated input should not return to step zero. Ableton's legato clip launch inherits the previous clip's play position; its gate, toggle and repeat launch modes also demonstrate why hold-to-play and latch-to-play need explicit semantics. [Ableton: Launching Clips](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/launching-clips/).

**Punch changes into a stable musical bed.** In Groove Fighter, a fill should be temporary, with an obvious return to the groove. An optional break can remove kick or hats for one beat rather than always adding notes. OP-Z punch-in effects are a concrete precedent for momentary performance transformations and separately editable performance capture. Our ordinary-note export contract means baking the resulting note changes into the take, unless a future schema explicitly supports effects. [Teenage Engineering: OP-Z Punch-In Effects](https://teenage.engineering/guides/op-z/punch-in-effects).

**Continuous input still needs discrete musical decisions.** Ribbon Lead should have a stable pitch region, a small hysteresis band around each region, bounded register and a deliberate rest/release action. Holding still must be meaningful. Starting with quantized note steps is honest: the current note schema can preserve pitches and durations, but does not represent a continuous pitch-bend performance. Do not advertise a recorded glide until the stored score and playback engine actually retain it.

## Concrete player scenarios

**Phrase Rider:** a beginner starts backing playback, chooses an arch motif and a soft instrument, holds for two bars, then lifts the phrase and increases intensity for the third. They settle and release in bar four. Success means the result sounds like one developing idea, and the player can predict the next repetition. A release should leave room in the backing; a quiet phrase should still have a complete shape.

**Groove Fighter:** the player starts a sparse backbeat, adds hats for a build, requests a last-beat fill, then hears the same groove return on the next downbeat. Success means the kick/snare identity remains clear and the fill is local. A drum audition needs actual kick/snare/hat differentiation; sending three MIDI pitches to one undifferentiated noise patch is a notation demo, not a convincing kit.

**Ribbon Lead:** the player holds a comfortable pitch, sweeps upward through a few scale regions, holds a high note, releases for a beat, then descends to an accompaniment anchor. Success means held regions remain stable, rapid pointer events do not create a cloud of notes, and the saved take reproduces what was auditioned. Offer equivalent arrow-key pitch and a gate key so pointer dexterity is not a prerequisite.

## Audition, record and backing workflow

Use a legible state sequence: **Stopped → Playing / audition → Recording → Take ready**. “Play backing” starts the shared clock and lets the player rehearse. “Record take” arms the next loop boundary with visible count-in; the existing song continues. “Stop recording” ends capture without unexpectedly killing backing playback. Show bar/beat and the queued boundary so a quantized response does not look broken. Count-in and playing along with existing clips are established recording workflows. [Ableton: Recording New Clips](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/recording-new-clips/).

Show which part is being performed and whether backing includes its existing notes. Offer a clear audition choice such as “Full song” versus “Backing without this part”; otherwise a second lead or drum groove can sound bad for reasons unrelated to the experiment. Harmonic guidance must use the same audible-track eligibility as playback. In a first scoped increment, documenting full-song backing is more honest than silently pretending that part subtraction exists.

After capture, provide Replay with backing, Keep as a new part, Replace selected part, and Discard; preserve the auditioned notes and patches exactly. Audition must not silently create a take. A rolling “keep what I just played” buffer is a useful later alternative to record anxiety, but needs explicit buffer bounds and recovery semantics. Ableton Capture MIDI retrieves material played on monitored tracks, supporting this as a concrete future precedent. [Ableton: Capturing MIDI](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/recording-new-clips/#capturing-midi).

## What to cut and test first

Cut combat scoring, automatic chord labels, tempo changes, random generation on every hit, complex combo vocabularies and simultaneous multi-part recording from the first trial. Give each experiment a modest set of contrasting presets and one obvious silence control. Instrument choice should alter timbre without silently selecting an unrelated composition mode.

Listen to each mode over: a simple major progression, a natural-minor song, sparse bass-only backing, a chromatic/foreign-note passage, and silence. Include a nonzero capture start and a short final bar. For non-4/4 meter, derive a bar from `numerator × PPQ × 4 / denominator`; a hard-coded four-quarter span must be labeled honestly or rejected for that meter. Scale metadata, sounding note snapshots and nearest-pitch rules cannot guarantee genre, tonal function, voice-leading quality or stylistic compatibility.

Ask a novice to repeat a recognizable idea, change energy, insert one rest, change instrument and keep a take; ask a musician to identify glitches, awkward register jumps, groove failures and clashes. Record whether the player can predict and deliberately reproduce a result, not just whether they like a preset. Compare original gestures and experiments on the same backing and patch. Automated gates should check timing, no duplicate scheduled notes, boundary handling, finite/ranged pitches, immutable inputs, sustained-input cleanup and exact audition-to-take equivalence. Human headphone/speaker listening remains required for musical quality.
